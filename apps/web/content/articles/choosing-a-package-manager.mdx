## The Short Answer

Most "npm vs Yarn vs pnpm" posts benchmark one project and declare a winner. That approach cannot work, because **the ranking changes with project size.**

I measured it: [pm-benchmark](https://github.com/arun9483/pm-benchmark) — four arms (npm, pnpm, Yarn with PnP, Yarn with `node-modules`) across six fixtures from 79 to 561 packages, against a pinned offline registry. Three independent passes, 10 runs per cell, **30 samples behind every number**.

> **The verdict, from run `20260815-0851Z-main`: use pnpm.** Not because it is fastest — it posts 8 wins, 3 ties _and_ 8 last places across the 6 fixtures. It wins because it is level with npm on speed (within 10 %) and decisively ahead on disk and defaults. Yarn PnP measured fastest overall but is ruled out on compatibility unless you own the entire toolchain.

That is the default. The rest of this article is when to deviate from it.

| Your situation                               | Choose                  | Why                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| You control the whole toolchain              | **Yarn PnP**            | 2.6–4.7× faster on warm and CI installs — won every fixture |
| Large projects, or many repos on one machine | **pnpm**                | Five copies of a Next.js app: **886 MB vs npm's 2.9 GB**    |
| Small projects, or you value zero surprises  | **npm**                 | No compatibility caveats, no config traps                   |
| You need `node_modules` on disk              | **pnpm**, not `yarn-nm` | Yarn without PnP is mid-pack or last nearly everywhere      |

## The Catch With Yarn PnP

It is decisively the fastest, and the cost is real: **plain `node` cannot resolve dependencies** — on any fixture. Anything shelling out to `node`, or expecting a `node_modules` directory, must be routed through Yarn. That is a toolchain-wide commitment, not a flag. Evaluate it against your own tooling before adopting.

## The Crossover Nobody Benchmarks

npm and pnpm **swap places** somewhere between ~100 and ~600 packages. On warm installs pnpm is _last_ on a 79-package library and _second_ on a 561-package app, while npm goes the other way — 2.76× to 4.72×.

The cleanest evidence holds composition fixed: `large500` is literally `mid300` plus `jest`. Within that one lineage, pnpm improves from 4.26× to 3.50× and npm degrades from 3.80× to 4.34×. **This is why a single-fixture benchmark tells you nothing** about a project that isn't the same size as the fixture.

## Trust Your Own Numbers More Than Mine

One finding matters beyond package managers. Running the identical suite three times, the winning arm **changed in 2 of 24 scenarios** — and cold installs varied ~4× more _between_ passes than _within_ one. The worst cell looked accurate to 0.89 % inside a single pass and moved 10.86 % across passes.

Single-pass benchmarks — including most published comparisons — report error bars that describe only the noise they could observe. If a gap is smaller than that, it is not a ranking.

Absolute seconds belong to my machine; the ratios are what travel. The full methodology, raw data and every caveat live in [REPORT.md](https://github.com/arun9483/pm-benchmark/blob/main/REPORT.md), and `make run N=3` reproduces the lot.
